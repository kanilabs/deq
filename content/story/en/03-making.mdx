## Ink

The ink most sources describe is soot mixed with the breast milk of a mother who has a daughter <Cite ids="dailysabah-2016 kurdistan-chronicle-2026 aljazeera-2023 amargi-2025 indyturk-2020 ozgur-2021 natakallam-2024" />.

Beyond that, the recipes differ. National Geographic and Daily Sabah add liquid from a sheep's or goat's gallbladder <Cite ids="natgeo-2015 dailysabah-2016" />, and Kurdistan Chronicle names ox bile or herbs <Cite ids="kurdistan-chronicle-2026" />. Other sources name herbs, lamp soot and animal intestines <Cite ids="wikipedia-deq aljazeera-2023 natakallam-2024" />, and in Kobanî and Shingal tree stems and milk were used <Cite ids="diplomatic-xal" />.

## Needle and skin

The marks were pricked with a sewing needle (_derzî_); the wound scabbed over and the mark turned greenish <Cite ids="indyturk-2020" />. Girls were tattooed between about 8 and 12 years old <Cite ids="natgeo-2015" />.

## The tattooers

The sources describe the tattooers in different words: travelling, nomadic or "gypsy" women <Cite ids="natgeo-2015" />, Dom women <Cite ids="aljazeera-2023" />, _Qeraçî_ <Cite ids="indyturk-2020" /> and Romani deqqars <Cite ids="amargi-2025" />. These names are not interchangeable, so each is given here as its source gives it.

## Where on the body

Women wore deq on the chin, hands, nostrils, forehead, neck, ankles and breasts <Cite ids="dailysabah-2016" />; in Karacadağ and Çınar, on the forehead, chin, ankle, hand and upper chest <Cite ids="ozgur-2021" />. Men wore it on the arms and hands <Cite ids="dailysabah-2016" /> and on the temples <Cite ids="ozgur-2021 diplomatic-xal" />.
